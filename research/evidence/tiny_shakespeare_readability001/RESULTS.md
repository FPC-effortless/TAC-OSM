# Tiny Shakespeare nine-checkpoint readability diagnostic

## Outcome

Within this small matched generation sample, **TAC-OSM four-layer seed 10601
is the strongest overall checkpoint**: it has the lowest recorded held-out
next-byte loss and the highest generated corpus-vocabulary rate. Four-layer
seed 10603 produced some of the clearest isolated dialogue fragments and the
highest recorded next-byte accuracy, but it was less consistent on generated
speaker labels. The Transformer family is the clear CPU decode-speed winner.

None of the nine checkpoints reliably produced a semantically coherent
1024-byte scene. All models reproduced the surface form of Shakespearean
dialogue while losing grammar, character identity, and scene continuity.
This is an exploratory system-behaviour observation, not an architecture
superiority claim.

## Matched protocol

Every checkpoint generated two 1024-byte continuations with identical
settings:

- prompts: `ROMEO:\n` and `KING LEAR:\nWhat have you done?\n`
- temperature: 0.7
- top-k: 40
- sampling seed: 1234
- device: CPU
- total: 18 passages and 18,432 generated bytes

All nine checkpoint files passed SHA-256 receipt verification before this
diagnostic. Model loading used PyTorch `weights_only=True` and strict state
dictionary loading. Generation used carried recurrent state/cache rather than
re-prefilling the full continuation. No weights changed.

## Quantitative comparison

| Model | Corpus words | Valid speaker labels | Recorded NLL ↓ | Recorded accuracy ↑ | Original CPU decode ↓ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **TAC-4 10601** | **94.3%** | 85.7% | **1.616** | 52.15% | 4.83 ms/byte |
| TAC-4 10603 | 93.3% | 76.5% | 1.621 | **52.34%** | 4.87 ms/byte |
| TAC-4 10602 | 93.3% | **88.9%** | 1.634 | 52.16% | 4.82 ms/byte |
| TAC-8 10603 | 91.5% | 58.3% | 1.678 | 50.86% | 9.35 ms/byte |
| TAC-8 10601 | 88.7% | 54.5% | 1.686 | 50.73% | 9.33 ms/byte |
| TAC-8 10602 | 88.2% | 42.9% | 1.700 | 50.10% | 9.59 ms/byte |
| Transformer 10603 | 88.9% | 72.2% | 1.709 | 49.17% | **1.50 ms/byte** |
| Transformer 10601 | 86.8% | 45.5% | **1.697** | **49.26%** | 1.50 ms/byte |
| Transformer 10602 | 86.1% | 61.5% | 1.728 | 48.65% | 1.52 ms/byte |

The generated-text measures contain about 390–417 word tokens and 7–18
speaker labels per model. Speaker-label percentages therefore have large
sampling uncertainty. Corpus-vocabulary rate is a spelling/lexical diagnostic,
not a semantic or grammatical score; a nonsensical sequence of real words can
score highly.

Family means make the tradeoff clearer:

| Family | Corpus words | Valid speaker labels | Recorded NLL ↓ | Recorded accuracy ↑ | Decode ↓ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| TAC, four layers | **93.6%** | **83.7%** | **1.623** | **52.22%** | 4.84 ms/byte |
| TAC, eight layers | 89.5% | 51.9% | 1.688 | 50.56% | 9.43 ms/byte |
| Transformer | 87.2% | 59.7% | 1.711 | 49.03% | **1.51 ms/byte** |

At the same approximately one-million-parameter scale and 4,096 recorded
updates, moving TAC from four to eight layers did not improve these metrics.
The eight-layer family was roughly twice as slow as four-layer TAC. This
diagnostic does not identify whether the regression comes from depth/width
allocation, optimization difficulty, or another training difference.

The Transformer decoded about 3.2 times faster than four-layer TAC and about
6.2 times faster than eight-layer TAC in the frozen CPU fixture. Its matched
samples were more varied but contained more malformed words and did not sustain
better scene-level meaning.

## Sampling sensitivity

The strongest recorded TAC and Transformer checkpoints were also generated
from `ROMEO:\n` at three temperatures, holding top-k, seed, and 1024-byte
length fixed:

| Setting | TAC-4 10601 | Transformer 10601 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature 0.0 | 100% corpus words; **91.6% repeated bigrams** | 100% corpus words; **97.4% repeated bigrams** |
| Temperature 0.4 | 99.5% corpus words; 15.6% repeated bigrams | 97.2% corpus words; 11.1% repeated bigrams |
| Temperature 0.7 | 93.1% corpus words; 3.5% repeated bigrams | 89.4% corpus words; 1.0% repeated bigrams |

Greedy decoding collapsed both models into loops. Lower temperature reduced
invented words but retained broken syntax. Higher temperature reduced local
repetition while increasing malformed words. The Transformer’s lower
repetition can make an isolated sample feel more fluent even when close reading
shows weak grammar and continuity.

## Provisional human-reading order

The following order summarizes the two matched passages per checkpoint. It is
post-hoc, subjective, and too small to support a statistical ranking:

1. TAC-4 10601 — best consistency across recorded loss and lexical validity
2. TAC-4 10603 — strongest occasional short exchange
3. TAC-4 10602 — good label structure, more local repetition
4. TAC-8 10603 — strongest eight-layer checkpoint
5. Transformer 10603 — best Transformer lexical/label behaviour in these samples
6. Transformer 10601 — best Transformer recorded NLL and accuracy
7. TAC-8 10601
8. TAC-8 10602
9. Transformer 10602

Positions in the middle are especially unstable. A credible semantic ranking
would require a preregistered prompt set, multiple sampling seeds, blinded
human raters or a validated external semantic rubric, and uncertainty over
prompt and sampling variation.

## Claim boundary

- Internally measured; not independently qualified.
- Post-hoc prompt and metric selection; not preregistered.
- Tiny Shakespeare only; no real-world capability, commercial, safety, or
  general-language claim.
- Reuses recorded held-out metrics; no protected holdout data or labels were
  newly opened during this diagnostic.
- Checkpoint parameter counts are close, but training-FLOP, tuning, and full
  selection parity were not independently re-audited here.
- No checkpoint promotion or architecture-superiority conclusion is eligible.
